# The writer's brief

You are about to tell one person something true about their life. They are sitting across from you. They are living this energy right now, and they are someone who can hold it. They have never heard of the I Ching or the Gene Keys or Human Design, and they are not going to. They are tired and listening, and you have one chance to be understood.

Every sentence on the card is something you would say to that person, out loud, and be understood the first time. Say it before you write it. Then choose the words.

That is the whole brief. What follows is how, then the few facts the card cannot do without. The reasoning is in `todo/plans/writing-guideline/fable-voice-pass.md`.

## First, understand it

Read the packet at `oracle/_packets/NN.md` whole, card 3 at `oracle/cards/03.md` once, and card 1 (`oracle/cards/01.md`) once more slowly: it is the first card written whole under this brief. Then shut everything and tell a friend what this energy is at its lowest and its height, in its situation, in the body, among its kin. If you cannot say it plainly you do not have it yet.

## How a sentence is made

The plain thing first, in the words you would use across the table. "The days go flat." Then, if it helps, one picture, and let the next sentence say what the picture was for. The plain sentence governs and the picture serves it. A picture the reader has to decode is not felt.

Give the sentence a subject that can be seen or felt and a verb that is what the thing really does; when the subject is "this energy" and the verb is "is", nothing appears. Put the weight at the end, because the last word is the one that stays. Keep the connecting words in, and the commas where you would breathe; a whole thought is sometimes twenty words long. What you cut is the list of nouns and the second adjective, never the breath.

Choose pictures the reader has touched with their own hands: a hand, a chest, morning, a grey week, rain, fire, ground, a child, an old person. These cross every border. An idiom does not, and a system's furniture does not, so the dragon stays in the I Ching section; everywhere else the card says rises.

Then choose the words. On the first pass you say it. On the second you go word by word and ask whether this is the word or only the first that came. Choosing is mostly cutting: the second adjective, the word put in to sound like an oracle, the sentence that says what the paragraph meant. Subtraction, never compression. Compression makes riddles.

Read every paragraph aloud and ask what appeared; if nothing did, say it to the person in your own words and write that down. And in every section find the one sentence that says a thing the reader has lived and never put into words: "You get to the end of a day tired and cannot say what the day was." Exactness about something ordinary is where the power is.

## Who is speaking, to whom

You, to you. Never "a person", never "the reader", never "this card" or "this reading" or "the deck". The card never talks about itself and never says where anything came from; the picture and the counsel are yours to give.

Name the thing, and name the energy itself. "What begins things in you rises on its own" asks the reader to hold an unnamed something through a whole essence, and "the wanting" names a smaller thing standing in for the energy; both failed on card 1. Say what it is in ordinary words in the first breath ("This energy comes in waves"), give the one picture whole so its verbs do not float (a wave, not "rises" and "goes under" from a picture never shown), and speak from the day the person lives, never from the mechanism. Adrian's essence for card 1 is the model: "This energy comes in waves, and between the waves there is nothing. No matter how hard you try, the wave returns only on its own. Every time it returns, it is the beginning of something new."

Enter before you speak. The first sentence of a section tells the person what you are about to talk about, the way anyone begins: "This is the energy that begins things." Then the rest. A section whose first sentence assumes they already know what you mean is a conversation joined in the middle, and on card 1 Adrian read it that way. The entry is different on every card and every section; what stays is that there is one.

Choose the verb. "A force that begins things is up in you" is a nondescript verb where the sentence needed its one live word; "is alive in you" is the same sentence chosen. An opening sentence above all carries no monotonous, indescript word; it is the first thing felt.

The person across the table is someone able to hold this energy, and the centre of gravity of every section is what it is to carry it well. They may not be in the difficult state at all. When the challenges come, the card lets them understand what those are turning them into and how they are supporting them; that is a quiet note under the writing, not its subject. What the card never does is run the formula "here is your challenge, here is the solution," and it never accuses. "You are numb" is a verdict. "Numbness is a feeling, though almost nobody knows they are in it" can be recognised, and the reader is still standing after it.

No system word in prose. Hexagram, trigram, gate, centre, shadow, gift, siddhi are labels the page shows and explains once; the prose says "at its lowest" or "the part of you that". The page shows the card name, the Gene Keys names, the organ and the amino acid; the prose never re-announces them. The essence is shown once, under the keynotes, and never repeated on the card. Never the em dash character: comma, colon, full stop, or a new sentence.

## One energy, six voices

Each panel answers one question only it can answer. The reading: what is this energy, and how do I stand in it? I Ching: what is the situation, and what does it counsel? Gene Keys: what does this energy become, at its lowest and its highest? Human Design: where does it press in me, and what does it drive me to do? Body: what is my body doing while I feel this? Relations: who else carries this, and what does it become when it meets its kin?

The I Ching's scene governs the card: the sprout, the mare, the dragon. ICHING shows it whole. The moving lines vary it six times and the Gene Keys vary it by height. DESIGN and BODY answer a question it raised. RELATIONS calls it back once, in the Tarot's lead sentence. CODE may hold one glimpse of it in plain clothes. No section teaches a fact another section teaches.

**The reading.** Three paragraphs, written last, from the essence. The first is the energy as a person holds it in balance, shown alone after a throw, so it must stand whole by itself. The second brings in the pull this cycle carries, as one plain picture, in the way the card's own material makes natural, without turning into a problem and its fix. The third is the way, ending a beat early. Nothing the sections say later is said here first.

**I Ching.** The Combination is the picture the two forces make, with no "you" in it. Each trigram is the force as itself, in the world, in a person, and its risk, the same on every card that carries it. The Reading is the situation as a person lives it, what it asks, the body once (Heaven the head, Earth the belly, Thunder the foot, Wind the thigh, Water the ear, Fire the eye, Mountain the hand, Lake the mouth), and the posture. The Judgement and Image speak as the classical text, rendered fresh. Each moving line stands alone, because it is read alone after a throw: its scene as a person lives it, what it asks, the counsel last.

**Gene Keys.** One energy at three heights. At its lowest it is under the ground and moves only when it is felt instead of pushed down. When it is met you stop fighting it and stand with it, and it starts to give. At its height it is no longer held as yours; it moves through you. That arc shapes every paragraph and is never named: seed, plant and flower do not appear. Each height opens on what is present and stands on its own, then names the height below as what it is made from. One picture across the three heights, the one the source uses. The two natures are two whole people, one who goes still and one who lashes out, named from the reference row's frontmatter, never inferred.

**Human Design.** This is the lens of this energy, never the energy itself. It may name and explain its gate, centre and channel in body terms, inside sentences that do work. It says what defined and open are like and never which the reader is; it never claims the chart and never tells the reader how to decide. It names the region and the pressure, never the organ or gland.

**Body.** One organ and one amino acid, from the frontmatter, and what each is doing while this energy is felt. Two paragraphs each: the thing, then the mechanism the reader can check inside a minute. Facts you could take into a kitchen, at their own scope, never advice, illness or healing. Body names the organ and what it does, never the centre.

**Relations.** One kin at a time, each paragraph standing alone: it names the kin by card name and places it in plain words in the same sentence, says what the two energies make together, and why you would open that card next. The one-line intro says what the kin have in common. Never the mechanics, never the reader's chart. Facts come from `relations_data`, never inferred.

## The order, and the sheet

Write ICHING, KEYS, DESIGN, BODY, RELATIONS, in that order. Then find the essence: three short whole sentences in `meta.centre`, the first naming the energy, the second its challenge as the reader feels it, the third its way, found from what the sections said. Then CODE from it. Then the keynotes, five to seven, in the card's own words.

The sheet lives under `meta:` in this order: `centre`; `scene` (the picture chosen, with what was set aside); `mechanism` (how the card holds together); `sources` (one list per section of the packet files its sentences trace to); then the folded blocks last, because the app stops reading `meta` at the first one (`lore` for anything written from established lore rather than a file; keep an existing `sourcing_note` or `fact_check` as history). Leave every `status:` as `scaffold`. Do not run builds, regenerate data, touch another card, or commit.

## Appendix: the file

`oracle/cards/NN.md`: frontmatter, then `## CODE`, `## ICHING`, `## KEYS`, `## DESIGN`, `## BODY`, `## RELATIONS`, in that order, each with its `###` subsections. Keep every heading exactly, em dash and tail included. The parser also reads: the `_Keywords:_` line; the Judgement and Image bullets; the `**Line N** · _image:_ ... → becomes Hexagram X, Name.` markers with their targets; the `_Shadow:_ · _Gift:_ · _Siddhi:_` line; the one-line underscore intro under RELATIONS; the `- **Label — Value:**` bullets under Tarot and Deeper correlation. Everything else is plain paragraphs: no bold, no italics, no lists, no quotes.

## What the checker measures, and what is retired

A script or a second pass, after the writing, checks: the parser structure above; the em dash in prose; "this card", "this reading", "the deck", "the oracle"; system words as terms of art; any source, book or teacher named; seed, plant and flower in KEYS; the natures' names against the reference row; the essence repeated verbatim; shared openings across cards (`measure.mjs`); and the punctuation numbers and word counts per section (`punctuation.mjs`), printed as information for the editor's eye, never as pass or fail.

Retired outright: the fifteen-words-a-sentence, commas-per-hundred and three-comma gates as gates; the one-long-one-short sentence rule; the thoughts-per-paragraph rule; the one-adjective rule; the three-sentence rule for the natures; the eighteen point check; and the retired-closer families. An editor with an ear catches what those were for. A writer counting them writes nothing worth counting.
