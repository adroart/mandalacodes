# The Judgement, the Image and the coins: what they are, and how a stranger meets them

Written 2026-09-12 after Adrian read card 3's I Ching panel on the live page and said a person with no exposure to the system has no idea what "The Judgement" and "The Image" mean, or what the coins are for. The stopgap labels ("The Judgement · the oracle's answer", "The Image · the picture to hold") did not help, and he asked whether "the oracle's answer" is even true. This file answers that, proposes the bridge, settles the order, and rewrites the coins copy. Read-only review; nothing in the card, the guideline or the component was touched.

Sources read: `oracle/GUIDELINE.md` sections 3, 5 and 7; `oracle/cards/03.md` ICHING section; the vault's hexagram 3 folder (Legge, Wilhelm, Huang, both Clearys, Deng, the Eranos oracle file); the panel markup in `components/oracle/eb/generated/EBReading.generated.tsx` and the cast logic in `EBReading.host.tsx` and `utils/ichingCasting.ts`. The Wilhelm introduction PDF in the Laser folder is the Clarity edition's how-to page, font-shifted, not Wilhelm's foreword; the vault's `wilhelm-intro.md` is an empty stub. Web checks on the Ten Wings, the tuan and the xiang were run through the built-in search (the i64os search endpoint is returning 401 today); they agreed with the vault and with what Huang says in his hexagram 3 chapter, and nothing below rests on the web alone.

## 1. What the Judgement and the Image were

**The Judgement.** The Chinese is 彖 tuàn. The character is a boar's head, and the sense that stuck to it is cutting, deciding, determining. Wilhelm's German was Das Urteil, which Cary Baynes turned into "The Judgment"; Legge left it as "the Thwan"; Huang calls it "the Decision"; Wilhelm himself, in his third book, calls the commentary on it "Commentary on the Decision". So "Judgement" is one translator's word for a Chinese word that means something closer to "the deciding". Strictly the text on the page is the guàcí, the hexagram statement, and 彖 is the name the commentary tradition gave it; every English reader meets it as the Judgement, and a reader who goes to any book after this card will find it under that name.

It is attributed to King Wen of Zhou, around 1050 BCE, held to have written the sixty-four statements while imprisoned by the last Shang king. Legge is blunt about what that means for reading it: King Wen "wrote, as he did in every hexagram, with reference to a particular state of affairs which he had in mind", the disorder around him and the destiny of his house. Huang reads hexagram 3's Judgement as a founder's memo to his sons: the dynasty is a sprout, be steadfast, take nothing lightly, set up the feudal lords who will hold the ground. That is where "put the helpers in their places" comes from.

What question it answered: the person had a situation and threw for it; the hexagram was the situation named; the Judgement was the tradition's ruling on that situation as a whole. Does it go well or badly, under what condition, and what should be done or not done. It carries the four ritual words (yuan heng li zhen: spring, growing, harvesting, trial in the Eranos reading; "supreme success, furthering through perseverance" in Wilhelm) and the plain instructions ("nothing should be undertaken", "it furthers one to appoint helpers"). When no line moves, the Judgement is the whole of what the throw says.

So is "the oracle's answer" true as a gloss? Half true, and the false half is the half a stranger hears. True: in a cast with no moving lines the Judgement is the ruling, the closest thing in the book to a verdict on a question. False, three ways. First, on this page nobody asked anything; the reader holds a card. An "answer" with no question is a fortune, which is the horoscope register section 5 forbids. Second, on this card the Reading above it is the wisdom, by guideline section 7's own rule; calling the block below it "the answer" tells the reader the Reading was not. Third, the Judgement does not answer a person, it rules on a situation, whoever is in it. The honest word is ruling, not answer.

**The Image.** The Chinese is 象 xiàng, which is the character for elephant and, by extension, figure, likeness, image, symbol. Legge translated it "Symbolism"; Wilhelm's Das Bild became Baynes's "The Image". On the page it is the Great Image, dà xiàng, one half of the Xiang Zhuan, the third and fourth of the Ten Wings. Tradition says Confucius wrote the Ten Wings; scholarship dates them to the Warring States period, two or three centuries after him, by Confucian hands. It is younger than the Judgement by five or six hundred years and is a different kind of text: commentary, not oracle.

It has one fixed shape for all sixty-four, and the shape is the whole point. First the two trigrams as a scene in nature ("Clouds and thunder"). Then "the noble one thereby" (jūnzǐ yǐ) and a rule of conduct drawn from that scene: brings order out of confusion (Wilhelm), plans and sets things in order (Huang), uses the canons to coordinate, which the Eranos philology unpacks as twisting fibres into a rope (the source of the card's "twist them into a cord that holds"). The question it answered was not the diviner's. It was the student's: given that the world is in this configuration, how does a cultivated person carry themselves in it. That is why it never says fortune or misfortune. It is a picture and a practice.

Modern readers use the two roughly as the Clarity and Wilhelm schools teach: read the Judgement for the situation and its verdict, read the Image for the attitude to hold, then the moving lines if any, then the hexagram they turn into. The Reading on this card already does the first two jobs in Adrian's voice; the two blocks are the tradition's own words underneath it.

## 2. Why they do not land for a stranger

The exact gaps, as the panel reads today.

The verdict register. "It goes well for the one who stays true" is a sentence in the shape of a fortune or a sentence passed, and a stranger reading a card about themselves takes it as one or the other. Nothing on the page has said that a ruling on a situation is what this is, so it lands either as flattery or as a threat.

The King Wen court context. "Put the helpers in their places" is "install feudal lords" from a man founding a state. Without the context it reads as management advice from nowhere; with the context it is a founder's instruction, and the card cannot give the context because section 7 bans the history book. The stranger has no way to hear it as anything but a strange imperative.

The scene-then-moral shape. "Clouds and thunder, the storm built and the rain not yet down. So the wise sort the loose strands." The "So" is the hinge, and it is invisible unless you know that the two trigrams are clouds and thunder and that the second sentence is meant to be drawn from the first. A stranger reads a weather line and then an unrelated instruction.

The compressed diction. The classical lines are telegrams: no subject, no connective, two to five lines each. Right after a Reading that speaks to you in whole sentences, the register drop is sharp and the reader has not been told why the voice changed.

The labels. "The Judgement" carries a courtroom and the Last Judgement; the vault folder for this very card holds a tarot file called Judgement, a different thing, and a stranger who knows tarot will hear that one. "The Image" on a page whose top is a photograph of a wooden sculpture means the photograph. The stopgap explainers made it worse: "the oracle's answer" promised a reply to a question never asked, and "the picture to hold" pointed the reader back to the artwork.

And the deepest gap: nothing tells the reader that these two blocks are older than the Reading, are the source it was drawn from, and are being quoted rather than spoken. Guideline section 3 already knows this ("the one place the tradition speaks rather than you being spoken to"); the page has not told the reader.

## 3. The bridge

The fix is the framing, not the lines. The classical lines on card 3 are rendered by the classical-text method, they are faithful to the six translations and the Eranos philology, they keep the "So" hinge that is the Image's whole structure, and they hold the register the guideline asks for. Rewriting them to explain themselves would break the one rule that makes them worth having: the tradition speaks, and does not annotate itself. What changes is the label line over each block, which is chrome, not prose, and is allowed to say what kind of thing follows.

The label keeps the classical name and the explainer does the translating, in at most eight plain words, saying what the block is and never who wrote it or when. Three candidate pairs.

Pair A. "The Judgement · the tradition's ruling on this situation" and "The Image · the scene, and how to act in it".

Pair B. "The ruling · the oldest words on this situation" and "The scene · what the wise do in it".

Pair C. "What the tradition rules" and "How the wise carry it", with no explainer, the label doing both jobs.

Pick Pair A. It keeps the names every book uses, so a reader who goes further recognises what they met here, and it answers the two questions a stranger actually has: what is this and why is the voice different. "Ruling" is what a judgement is and is true to 彖; "the tradition's" says who is speaking without a date or a name; "on this situation" ties it to the situation the Reading just described, not to a question. "The scene, and how to act in it" is the Image's fixed two-part shape stated as a shape, so the "So" hinge is expected before it arrives. Pair B loses the names and a reader who later opens Wilhelm cannot find the ruling. Pair C is clean but "carry it" is the deck's own idiom, not a stranger's, and it hides that the Image is a picture.

Two things about the rendering that are not rewrites. The Image's second line should always begin with its hinge word (So, Thus, Then) because the explainer has just promised that shape; card 3 does this and the rule should be written down. And the two blocks should be set so the eye knows they are quoted before it reads a word: the label above is one signal, the lines themselves are the other, and if the label is the only thing separating them from the Reading's voice, the rendering has failed the register test in section 3.

## 4. Placement

The panel today, top to bottom: the glyph, Combination, the two trigram texts, the Reading, the Judgement, the Image, then the coins. Adrian said "let's have the judgment be after". The Judgement is already after the Reading, so the sentence most plausibly means after the Image.

Read the panel as a stranger. The glyph and the three texts above the Reading say what this thing is made of. The Reading speaks to you and ends on the body and the posture ("ready, and staying"). What comes next should stay close to that: the Image is a picture the eye takes in one look, and its second line is conduct, which is exactly where the Reading just left off. Then the Judgement, the sharpest and oldest thing on the panel, the ruling itself. Then the coins. The ruling sits beside the coins on purpose: what the throw brings up, the moving lines, are the same age and the same family of voice as the Judgement (King Wen's son wrote them), and each one qualifies the ruling for the place you stand. So the reader hears the ruling, then throws to find which part of it is theirs. If the coins came before the two blocks the throw would end the panel: the cast, the turning lines and the "Read where it stands" link are a terminus, and anything under them goes unread.

Every book prints Judgement then Image. The page is not a book and its reader is a stranger; the deck can choose. Recommended order in one line: glyph, Combination, upper and lower trigram, the Reading, the Image, the Judgement, Cast the coins.

## 5. The coins

What the code does, measured: the card carries the hexagram, the coins only decide which of its six lines are in motion (one chance in four per line), the lines in motion are read alone with their own text, and flipping them gives the hexagram this one is turning into, which is another card in the deck. So the copy can say, truthfully, that the reader already holds this card and the throw asks where they are in it.

The current copy is forty-four words and leans on three things a stranger does not have: that the throw makes lines, that "turn" is a thing a line does, and what "the one it is becoming" means. Three candidates under forty words.

Candidate A. "Hold what is on your mind and throw. You already hold this card. The throw shows where you stand in it: the lines in motion, each with an old reading, and the card this one is becoming." Thirty-seven words. Keeps the invitation first; says nothing about what a throw is.

Candidate B. "This card is already yours. The throw asks where you are in it. Three coins, six times. Some of its lines land in motion. Each carries an old reading, and flipped, they show the card this one turns into." Thirty-nine words.

Candidate C. "Three coins, six times, make the six lines. Any that land in motion are where you stand in this card, each with an old reading, and flipped they show the card this one turns into. Hold your question. Throw." Thirty-nine words. Opens on the mechanic, which is a manual, and says the card is theirs only in passing.

Pick B. It answers the four things in the order a stranger needs them: this is already your card, so the throw is not a draw; what the throw is for; what physically happens; what comes back and what it means. "Its lines" points at the glyph the reader has already seen. "Old reading" says the line texts are the tradition's without a date. The pull is the second sentence, which is a question about you, not a description of a method. Rhythm: five, eight, three, six, seventeen words, a long one and a short one, not in the same place as the label lines above.

After the throw, in the same register. Over the bars, "Your throw", then the card's own name under them as now. When lines moved, the second beat reads "Turning into" over "Hexagram 8 · Holding Together" and the link under it says "Open that card". When none moved, one line: "No line is in motion. You stand in this card as it is." The moving-lines block is headed "The lines in motion", with one sentence under it: "Two of this card's six lines landed in motion. Each is a place you stand, with its old reading." Each line is labelled "Line 2 · in motion", then its scene line, then its text, then "Turned over, this line alone leads to Hexagram 60 · Limitation" when several moved and "Turned over, it leads to" when one did. The words to retire are "cast" as a noun for the result, "moving toward", "unstable", "flipped" as a label, and "Read where it stands", each of which names the mechanic instead of the reader.

## The rule this adds to guideline section 7

The frame explains; the text never does. The Judgement and the Image are the one place the tradition speaks on the card, so the classical lines carry no explanation of themselves and the Reading carries none of them; the explaining is done by the label line over each block, in at most eight plain words, saying what kind of thing follows (a ruling on this situation; a scene and how to act in it) and never who wrote it or when. The Image's second line always opens on its hinge word. The order is the Reading, then the Image, then the Judgement, then the coins, so the ruling sits beside the throw that qualifies it. The coins copy says what the throw does in the reader's terms (you already hold this card; the throw asks where you are in it; the lines in motion carry old readings; turned over they show the card this one turns into) and never the mechanism (six and nine, old yin, yarrow). The register test: cover the labels, and the Reading must still read as one person speaking to you while the two blocks read as something older being quoted; if only the labels tell them apart, the rendering has failed.
