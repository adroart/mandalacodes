# Are the 64 hexagram characters actually pictures

Fact-finding pass for the writing guideline. Question: for each of the 64 traditional
Chinese hexagram names, is the character genuinely a picture of the thing the name
means, so a card can say "the traditional character draws X" and be right. No card
prose here, just the verdicts and the reasoning behind them.

## Method

1. Grepped the vault's Eranos-derived source (Ritsema and Karcher, "Oracle 2018",
   at oracle/hexagrams/NN/oracle-NN-*.md) for the first "Ideogram:" gloss attached
   to the hexagram's own name term in its "Fields of meaning" section. Many
   hexagrams get no such gloss for the name term itself, even though the file has
   Ideogram glosses for other words in the judgment text. That absence is recorded
   as "no gloss" below, not filled in with a guess.
2. Read the "Name and Structure" opening of Alfred Huang's translation
   (huang-NN-*.md) for every hexagram, which usually gives its own, independent
   reading of the character's parts, and sometimes says outright that a component
   is there only for its sound.
3. Spot-checked 20 of the characters against Wiktionary's etymology sections
   (which draw on Schuessler, the Shuowen Jiezi, and modern oracle-bone
   scholarship), picked to cover cases where Eranos and Huang disagreed, where a
   gloss sounded like a folk story, and a spread of hexagrams across the deck.
   URLs are listed at the end.

Two things worth knowing before reading the table. First, the Eranos ideogram
glosses are Ritsema and Karcher's own English renderings of the traditional
component analysis you find in the Shuowen Jiezi (compiled circa 100 CE) and
similar sources, not modern paleography read off oracle bones. Traditional
component analysis is frequently wrong about what a character actually shows,
because the graph had already drifted a long way from its oldest form by the
time the Shuowen was written, and its author sometimes filled gaps with folk
etymology. Second, this means a component that provides only a character's
sound (a phonetic) can look, on paper, exactly like a component that
contributes meaning, and only comparison with oracle-bone or bronze forms tells
the two apart. That gap is where most of the false "picture" claims live.

## Verdict key

- **PICTURE** — the character genuinely draws the thing the hexagram name means.
- **COMPOUND** — the character's parts are individually meaningful and combine
  into a readable small scene, but that scene is not itself a drawing of the
  named thing (an abstract or relational meaning built from concrete parts).
- **PHONETIC** — the character is a sound-plus-meaning compound; the "picture"
  people report is a folk reading of what is structurally a phonetic component.
- **UNCLEAR** — the sources disagree, or the etymology is genuinely contested,
  or neither Eranos nor Huang offers a structural reading to check.

## Counts

- PICTURE: 9 (hexagrams 3, 18, 27, 42, 47, 48, 49, 50, 56)
- COMPOUND: 16 (hexagrams 5, 10, 11, 13, 14, 23, 24, 32, 34, 39, 40, 52, 54, 55, 58, 60)
- PHONETIC: 20 (hexagrams 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 15, 16, 17, 20, 21, 30, 33, 37, 38, 41, 44, 45, 51, 53)
- UNCLEAR: 19 (hexagrams 7, 9, 19, 22, 25, 26, 28, 29, 31, 35, 36, 43, 46, 57, 59, 61, 62, 63, 64)

Total 64. Fewer than one in seven hexagram names are a genuine picture of the thing
they name; roughly a third are sound-plus-meaning compounds wearing a folk-picture
story; the rest are either legitimate but non-literal compounds, or genuinely
unsettled.

## Strongest PICTURE cases — safe to say "the character draws X"

- **48, The Well (井)** — a well-frame or field-grid. The cleanest, best-attested
  pictograph in the set.
- **50, The Cauldron (鼎)** — a three- or four-legged bronze sacrificial vessel,
  drawn front-on.
- **47, Oppression (困)** — a tree fenced inside an enclosure.
- **42, Increase (益)** — a dish with water overflowing above it.
- **3, Difficulty at the Beginning (屯)** — a sprout bent as it breaks through
  the ground, root below.
- **18, Work on the Decayed (蠱)** — worms breeding inside a food vessel.
- **27, Nourishment (頤)** — an open jaw with teeth and food, drawn from the side
  (the character's other half is a sound-marker, not part of the picture).
- **56, The Wanderer (旅)** — people gathered beneath a banner.
- **49, Revolution (革)** — a stretched, flayed animal hide, head and legs still
  attached (confirmed independently of the vault sources).

## Traps — glosses that circulate as pictures but are not

- **37, The Family (家人)** — "roof over a pig" is the single most-repeated
  "obvious picture" character in popular writing about Chinese, and it is a folk
  reading: the pig-shaped component was originally a different word used only
  for its sound, and later graphic corrosion made it look like a pig.
- **30, The Clinging, Fire (離)** — Huang's own text says outright that the bird
  on the right supplies the character's sound, not its meaning; "Li symbolizes
  brightness, though that is not its literal meaning." The "magical fire-bird"
  story is a later semantic overlay on a sound-loan.
- **20, Contemplation (觀)** — the "watching bird" is a stork character borrowed
  purely for its sound; the actual meaning-bearing part is the eye/see component.
- **21, Biting Through (噬嗑)** — both halves of this two-character name are
  built from divination- and cover-shaped components chosen for their sound
  (shi, he), not for their meaning; Eranos's "revealing the essential" and
  "mouth and cover" readings dress up a phonetic pairing as a meaningful one.
- **16, Enthusiasm (豫)** and **44, Coming to Meet (姤)** — both get vivid
  Eranos/Huang component stories ("sonhood and elephant," "a queen greeting a
  king") built on components (予, 后) that function as sound-carriers.
- **6, Conflict (訟)** and **53, Development (漸)** — Huang gives an elaborate
  scene for each, but in both cases the non-water/speech half of the character
  is there for sound, a point his own text half-admits for 漸 ("provide the
  sound of the character Jian").
- **46, Pushing Upward (升)** — Huang's sun-and-grain-measure picture describes
  a different, related character; his own text notes the hexagram's actual name
  traditionally drops the sun component entirely, which undercuts his picture
  before it starts.
